Services

Three kinds of work, all of it local

The Great Black Swamp was drained by hand and by tile over the better part of a century. Almost every land use and drainage question in this region still traces back to that. Knowing the ground matters as much as knowing the software.

Land use analysis

Parcel and zoning work for planning commissions, township trustees, and county planners — including change over time, farmland conversion, and suitability for a proposed use.

  • Parcel & zoning inventories
  • Land cover change detection
  • Development suitability
  • Comprehensive plan mapping

911 & EMA coordination mapping

Response-side geospatial work for county EMA offices and 911 districts. The kind of data that has to be right the first time it's queried, at three in the morning.

  • Address point verification
  • Road centerline maintenance
  • Hazard & evacuation mapping
  • Response coverage analysis

Watershed mapping

Delineation and drainage analysis for soil and water conservation districts, county engineers, and watershed groups working a region defined by how its water leaves.

  • Watershed & subbasin delineation
  • Flow accumulation modeling
  • Ditch & tile network mapping
  • Runoff & nutrient context

A three-county land use plan built from scratch

There was no regional dataset to inherit. The base had to be assembled county by county — parcels and zoning from three separate county portals, road inventory from ODOT TIMS, demographics from Census TIGER/Line — and reconciled into layers that behaved consistently across county lines.

The cartography got the same treatment. Rather than three unrelated map series, I built one layout template that reskins per county: same grid, same typography, same legend and credit block, with only the header lockup, accent color, and county seal changing. The plan reads as a single document, and each board still sees a map that looks like theirs.

Finding the farmland when the county doesn't flag it

Wood County doesn't follow the standard Ohio DTE land use scheme — the 300-series is industrial rather than residential, and the 500-series functions as a tax exemption overlay rather than an institutional use. Every code had to be crosswalked to planning categories that a commission could actually read, with the ambiguous ones flagged rather than quietly assigned.

The CAUV question was harder. The parcel export carries no enrollment field, which the Auditor's office confirmed by phone; the only authoritative check is parcel-by-parcel on Beacon. So enrollment was derived from the reduced valuation, spot-checked against Beacon property reports, and the whole chain was written up on its own worksheet — because a number like 58.83% only survives a public meeting if you can show where it came from.

Reconciling 167 acres that didn't want to be reconciled

Existing land use and zoning, summarized citywide and broken out across seven planning areas and eighteen zoning districts — including the unincorporated township land inside the planning boundary but outside city jurisdiction, which is exactly the row that gets argued about.

The planning area polygons totaled about 167 acres more than the classified land use table. That gap is roads, water, and geometry slivers carrying no land use assignment. I tracked it down, established the correct denominator, and put a methodology note on the face of the spreadsheet explaining why the totals don't tie and why that's right rather than wrong.

Which parcels no longer fit the district they're in

Parcel-level work to identify non-conforming residential lots by zoning district, and to flag residentially-used parcels sitting inside non-residential districts — concentrated in the Park East and Old Ontario areas, where the zoning map and the built reality drifted apart decades ago.

One honest limitation was worth naming up front: CAMA data gives acreage but not lot dimensions or frontage, so conformity tests that depend on lot width can't be answered from the parcel table alone. Better to scope that at the start than to deliver a number nobody can stand behind.

Also: countywide land use classification for Sandusky County across 33,353 parcels, and CAUV enrollment analysis for Waterville Township — 175 parcels, roughly 5,907 acres.

Most of this work arrives as a formatted Excel workbook with a summary tab, a breakdown by planning area or district, and a methodology note written in plain English — plus the geodatabase or shapefile behind it. The point is that six months from now, somebody else can pick it up and see how the number was made.

About

Every map is an argument. I'd rather hand you one you can defend in a public meeting.

I've been doing geospatial work for close to ten years, counting the years I spent studying it. Black Swamp Analytics is the name I registered for it in August 2026 — the company is new, the work behind it isn't.

It's a one-person firm on purpose. The person who scopes your project is the person who writes the queries and the person who answers the phone when a commissioner has a question about slide fourteen. Nothing gets handed to a junior analyst you've never met, and nothing gets lost between them and me.

I named it after the Great Black Swamp because that's the ground I work on. Northwest Ohio's drainage history explains an enormous amount about why parcels sit where they do, why certain roads flood, and where a watershed boundary is going to surprise you. That context doesn't come out of a national dataset.
